# GxP AI panel on POC hell, ROI, and human oversight

> Recorded 2026-09-15 at 0909 via Plaud. 46:38.

## Summary

A four-speaker panel at the GxP and AI conference in Raleigh, moderated by Dori Gonzalez-Acevedo (CEO of ProcellaRX), with Gaurav Walia (VP at PQE Group International Consulting, a 30-year industry veteran, ex-Pfizer drug inventor, and former director of quality with over 100 FDA inspections and three consent decrees), Anant Agrawal (Head of Data at CIMCON Software, Carnegie Mellon-trained in statistics and machine learning), and Tim Roy Kirkelie (COO of Genari AI, from a manufacturing and regulatory QA background). Acevedo frames the arc as 2024 the idea year, 2025 the pilot year, and 2026 the year of judging outcomes, and opens by citing the Anthropic CEO's essay calling for a slowdown as reason for more nuanced conversation rather than retreat. On POC hell, Kirkelie describes AWS-funded proofs of concept from three years ago that went nowhere for lack of a long-term plan, a team that switched Jira to AI everywhere and slowed its development to a crawl, and a company he calls really good at this stuff that is still in POC hell; his biggest adoption fear is users just pressing the button to approve AI output. Walia prescribes a stepwise path: define the problem before the tech, plan the process and culture change, ensure quality readiness, then move POC to pilot to commercialization with the finish line defined first.

On ROI, Agrawal argues the investment is upfront while the benefit comes later, so vendors should prove value with the client's own data first, as a consulting engagement, before any tech buildout to avoid IT hell; she also calls accuracy percentages misleading, because 95 percent accurate still means a human must review 100 percent of the output, so ROI should be reframed as the time it takes humans to find the critical 5 percent of errors, supported by a broader error taxonomy covering omissions and conflicts with input data. On human-in-the-loop, Walia cites newly published brain-imaging work showing a decline in critical-thinking activity when people use AI, notes that with five signatures on a document probably two signers are not paying attention, and predicts a future state of continuous change control and continuous validation that still starts and ends with the human. On regulatory uncertainty, Acevedo, a co-author of a digital validation good practice guide, explains that Annex 22 is still in draft after its original no-probabilistic-AI stance drew pushback in a June meeting, asks why people trust AI with their bank account but hesitate over their drugs, argues cybersecurity is the bigger immediate risk (one organization's 190 security controls can only be managed with AI itself), and closes personally: her bipolar disclosure in front of 600 people at a Boston keynote, and two interns who arrived anti-AI and left ten weeks later integrating agents into their work. Algarwal, speaking as a vendor, recommends focusing on non-GxP, low-risk but high-ROI use cases now rather than waiting out uncertain regulation, says the 95-percent-of-AI-pilots-fail studies have holes in how they measure ROI, and pushes a reward-first mindset: reinvent whole processes together instead of each person getting 10 percent faster with Copilot.

2. **Adopt a "Reward-First, Problem-First" Mindset**: Start with a bold, optimistic vision for what AI can achieve or a well-defined business/quality problem, not the technology itself. Assess cultural readiness and process changes before piloting.
3. **Rethink ROI and Metrics**: ROI in GxP AI should be measured by end-to-end workflow impact, especially the reduction in human-review time needed to locate and correct critical errors, rather than misleading headline accuracy claims. 7. **Foster Collaboration and AI Literacy**: Successful integration requires breaking down organizational silos and fostering cross-functional literacy. Scientists, quality, and AI specialists must align on governance, and all employees need to understand how AI works to use it responsibly.
