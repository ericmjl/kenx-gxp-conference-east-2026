# Bharanitharan Rajendran on defensible AI governance and human risk in GxP

## Summary

A 44-minute conference lecture by Bharanitharan Rajendran, co-founder and CEO of Validfor, an AI-driven visual validation platform, on reducing human risk in GxP environments. His opening move is a paradox: the industry fears out-of-control AI while human error remains the actual citation engine. FDA drug warning letters rose 15.9 percent to 303 in the previous fiscal year, 21 CFR 211.22(d), failure to follow written procedures, was highlighted 184 times and has been the top recurring citation for four consecutive years, and a Nucleus study publicized by PwC attributes over 80 percent of process deviations to humans. Quality systems, he argues, are built on the assumption that humans will obey SOPs, which is hope rather than control, and the organization, not the AI, will always be the party holding the warning letter. He sorts the failures into six categories of human risk: documentation, investigation (root-causing everything as human error and answering it with retraining), change control impact assessment, quality oversight, data integrity (shared logins, backdated paper records, and inspection "war rooms"), and validation lifecycle treated as a one-time event. Enforcement stories follow: the April 2, 2026 first AI-related CGMP warning letter against Purolea Cosmetics Lab, where AI-generated SOPs went unreviewed and the firm answered an inspector's question about missing process validation with "AI did not tell us"; a vendor who deleted a clinical trial's data and audit trail, costing 47 subjects their data (Applied Therapeutics, Dec 2024); and a contract lab falsification with backdated records that exposed potentially hundreds of downstream pharma companies (Tentamus India, Mar 2026).

Against the "regulators are too slow" complaint he lays out a 2025 to 2026 convergence: FDA draft guidance on AI credibility assessment (Jan 2025), the ISPE GAMP AI guide and EU GMP draft Annex 22 (Jul 2025), the SeniorLife finding that intended use, not the marketing name, determines regulatory classification (Aug 2025), FDA/EMA joint Good AI Practice principles (Jan 2026), and the Apr 2026 warning letter, after which "all the excuses are gone." Defensible AI then gets five properties: documented intended use that states what the AI cannot do, risk-proportionate validation run as a continuous lifecycle because models drift, ALCOA+ audit trails extended to capture user, model and version, inputs, outputs, justification, and confidence score, enforced approval by a named accountable human (he reframes human-in-the-loop as human-centric design, since nobody genuinely reviews a hundred AI-generated documents), and continuous lifecycle monitoring. AI is "not a faster human" and not a compliance chatlet that drafts SOPs; in the control loop "AI acts, humans decide, and the system remembers everything." Industry examples: a Moderna colleague's personal four-step model quality approach (public model evaluation, platform internal benchmarking, use case, continuous quality monitoring) and Daiichi Sankyo's governed in-house generative AI environment against shadow-AI data leakage. The close is a three-step plan for tomorrow: inventory every AI touching GxP including shadow AI with a context-of-use statement, enforce a human gate so no AI output enters a GxP record without named review and approval, and treat validation as a continuous lifecycle, ending on "untrained humans fail inspections, and untrained AI fails patients." Q&A covers where intended use is documented (a standalone AI description or inside the existing validation plan; the point is that it is documented), what software must change so quality reviewers can sign off knowledgeably (review cycles, decisions, and rationales captured inside the system instead of offline chats and email), and how to control fraud and falsification rooted in culture and fear of losing jobs, where his answer begins with culture before the recording cuts off mid-sentence.

### 1. Human Risk vs. AI Risk in GXP

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">3. Six Categories of Human Risk</mark>

- **Documentation Failure:** Incomplete or inconsistent records, often discovered only during FDA inspections.
- **Investigation Failure:** Repeatedly citing "human error" as a root cause and using retraining as an ineffective solution.
- **Change Control Failure:** Incomplete downstream impact assessments of changes.
- **Oversight Failure:** Quality units missing critical issues due to pressure and reliance on manual tools.
- **Data Integrity Failure:** Sharing logins, backdating records, and not using audit trails.
- **Validation Lifecycle Failure:** Treating validation as a one-time event, allowing performance drift to go undetected until an inspection.
[](plaud://image?type=summaryCard&id=oc_su_2ef2cc13e3a9bb5020bf19b33673e72f_i9)

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">4. Five Properties of Defensible AI Control</mark>

- **Documented Intended Use:** Clearly define what the AI can and cannot do to set the scope for validation.
- **Risk-Proportionate Validation:** Conduct AI-specific risk assessments and align validation rigor with risk. Validation must be continuous to address model drift and evolving performance.
- **ALCOA+ / Secure, Non-Overwriting Auditability:** Audit trails must be immutable and capture comprehensive details: user, AI model/version, inputs, outputs, rationale, and confidence score.
- **Enforced Human-in-the-Loop Oversight:** A named, credentialed human must review and approve AI outputs, retaining full accountability.
- **Continuous Lifecycle Monitoring:** Move beyond periodic reviews to ongoing surveillance to detect drift and maintain a validated state.
[](plaud://image?type=summaryCard&id=oc_su_2ef2cc13e3a9bb5020bf19b33673e72f_i10)

### 5. Defensible AI Governance and Architecture

- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">**Defensible AI Control Loop:** An architecture where "AI acts, humans decide, and the system remembers everything."</mark> AI has capability but no authority; authority resides with the human quality unit. This requires immutable records, verifiable checksums, and enforced QA gates.
- **Human-Centric Design:** Go beyond superficial "human in the loop" checks by embedding named user accountability and meaningful review workflows into the system.
- **Traceability and Audit Readiness:** Ensure all records (plans, protocols, data) are traceable to their origin and context. Systems must lock data, maintain immutable logs, and provide inspection-ready artifacts.

### 6. Three-Step AI Governance Model

- **Step 1: Inventory Your AI:** List every AI tool touching a GxP process, including "shadow AI," and write a clear context-of-use statement for each.
- **Step 2: Enforce the Human Gate:** Mandate that no AI output enters a GxP record without review and approval by a named human. This is a governance decision that can be implemented immediately.
- **Step 3: Treat Validation as a Lifecycle:** Implement continuous monitoring for every AI system to manage drift, performance, retraining, and change control.
[](plaud://image?type=summaryCard&id=oc_su_2ef2cc13e3a9bb5020bf19b33673e72f_i11)

### 7. Inspection-Readiness and the Role of Quality Professionals

- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">**Core Inspection Questions:** Be prepared to answer: Do you use AI?</mark> How do you validate its outputs? Who in your quality unit is responsible? When did you last review its performance?
- **Division of Labor:** Delegate preliminary checks (e.g., typos) to AI, allowing quality professionals to focus on high-level decisions like evaluating deviations, verifying root causes, and approving corrective actions.
- **Cultural Controls:** Recognize that fraud and falsification are often rooted in organizational culture. Enforce immutable records, non-bypassable QA gates, and clear accountability to deter misconduct.
