# Abhishek Bachchan on AI acceptance criteria in GxP-regulated systems

> Recorded 2026-09-15 at 1426 via Plaud. 43:29.

## Summary

Talk recorded at the GxP and AI conference Eric is attending this week. The presenter, Abhishek Bachchan (Gilead (transcribed as "Brilliant Sciences"), IT security, risk and compliance), opens with why traditional CSV-style validation breaks for AI: deterministic systems return the same output for the same input and bugs reproduce consistently, while a generative system can satisfy one requirement in many ways, vary from run to run, and a human judging pass or fail quietly moves the acceptance criterion into the tester. His reframe is that validating an AI system means validating a measurement system: criteria must name a population (a versioned, change-controlled labeled test set), a risk-justified threshold, stated conditions (model version, corpus, parameters), a named harness at a specific commit, and a deviation route for failures, with dated run records rather than screenshots as evidence. He situates this in FDA SaMD guidance (locked vs adaptive algorithms, with adaptive ones needing a predetermined change control plan), the EU GMP Annex 22 draft requiring human oversight for continuously learning and generative models, and the joint FDA/EMA principles, where roughly four of ten principles touch acceptance criteria or validation. Both CSV and CSA still assume determinism, a human error-catcher, and a validated state that persists without action, so neither is sufficient for AI; he classifies AI systems on two axes, fixed vs adaptive and opaque vs inspectable, and focuses on the adaptive-but-inspectable quadrant, the dominant Gen AI use case, where prompts, responses, and performance can be tuned and monitored.

The practical core is a taxonomy of six acceptance-test buckets (retrieval of the right source, faithfulness to retrieved sources, reproducibility, boundary refusal, human-in-the-loop, and guardrail or authority respect), demonstrated on his own RAG system for chatting about regulations, warning letters, and inspections: close to a hundred labels including roughly nine drug-vs-device distractors, follow-up questions, and known-absent items to probe hallucination, with hallucination metrics catching eight failures that tuning pushed to about 99 percent. The inspection-ready validation package is a dashboard or database rather than a PDF: a labeled test set with rationales, run records, per-call provenance (requested vs served model, e.g. Opus for generation and Haiku for cheap comparisons, prompt hashes, token counts), a grounding exception log, stored output artifacts, human review dispositions, threshold rationale, and requalification records. Evidence has a shelf life: any trigger (model or provider change, prompt tweak, corpus growth, retrieval or chunking parameter changes, context-of-use shift, elapsed time) forces re-measurement in a continuous loop rather than the one-pass V model. On LLM-as-judge he cites research showing only about 76 percent agreement and quickly picked-up biases, so it belongs in calibrated triage, not acceptance evidence. Audience Q&A covers what gets handed over (a dashboard, not a zip file or PDF), the workload cost of human-in-the-loop review, and a cardiologist anecdote where AI helped a cardiac-trained doctor go from wrong 90 percent of the time on non-cardiac cases to right 70 percent of the time; Abhishek's closing point is that AI will not get sued, the human will, so the human stays in the loop.

## Knowledge Points

1. Why traditional validation breaks for AI
   - Expected-result vagueness: generative systems can satisfy requirements in multiple valid ways and vary across runs, defeating single-answer pass/fail checks and enabling tester subjectivity.
   - Stochastic behavior: bugs may not reproduce; “pass” means performance meets a distributional threshold over a named population; evidence must be dated runs over controlled sets, not screenshots; regression requires re-measuring distributions.
2. The reframe: validate the measurement system
   - Population + threshold: acceptance criteria must name the evaluated population and a risk-calibrated threshold (n cases, one metric, one number to beat).
   - Controlled test sets: cases and labels are the specification; place under change control and versioning to preserve comparability.
   - Evidence as run records: capture date, harness version/commit, corpus size/composition, parameters, per-case rows, scores, and operator identity; evidence is a distribution with provenance, not a static artifact.
3. Regulatory landscape and risk differentiation (as of Sep 2026)
   - FDA SaMD: “locked” vs. “adaptive”; adaptive requires a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) governing updates, testing, and documentation.
   - EU GMP Annex 22 (draft) and EMA: higher scrutiny and human oversight for adaptive/generative models; finalization targeted Q4 2026 (verify status).
   - In-force and guidance: FDA/EMA joint Good AI Practice (non-binding), FDA draft on AI in regulatory decisions (pending final), ISPE GAMP AI Guide (2025) and GAMP 5 2nd Ed. Appendix D11.
   - Enforcement basis: binding clauses (21 CFR 11, 211.68, 211.100; EU Annex 11; 21 CFR 820) apply without naming AI; inspectors cite procedures/records/validation, not “the model was wrong.”
4. Acceptance criteria within FDA/EMA principles
   - Risk-based approach, clear context of use, risk-based performance assessment, and lifecycle management (scheduled monitoring and drift re-evaluation).
   - Human-centric design: interpretability, explainability, and plain-language performance/limitations; acceptance criteria must reflect human oversight.
5. CSV vs. CSA vs. AI validation
   - CSV: scripted expected results for deterministic systems.
   - CSA: risk-based assurance, recognizes unscripted testing; still assumes determinism.
   - AI validation: measure populations against declared thresholds; maintain continuous monitoring and re-qualification; treat provider changes and drift as triggers to re-measure.
6. Gaps CSA doesn’t solve for AI
   - Single expected result vs. distributions; intermittent failures; effort sized by consequence of error; maintaining a validated state amid silent provider changes.
   - Do not substitute vendor evidence; qualify evaluation harnesses; detect provider model reroutes/parameter shifts via monitoring.
7. Classification framework for AI behavior
   - Axes: Behavioral Stability (Fixed vs. Adaptive) and Decision Transparency (Inspectable vs. Opaque).
   - Quadrants:
     - Fixed & Inspectable: classic validation suffices.
     - Fixed & Opaque: test behavior; no logic to review.
     - Adaptive & Inspectable: preferred “honest middle ground” enabling oversight.
     - Adaptive & Opaque: hardest; validate behavior on schedule indefinitely.
8. Anatomy of a defensible acceptance criterion
   - Structure: “On [labeled set version under change control], the system shall achieve [metric] ≥ [threshold] under [conditions], measured by [named harness at commit], with failures dispositioned via [deviation route].”
   - Components: versioned labeled set; fit-for-use metric aligned to failure modes; threshold justified by consequence of failure; explicit conditions (model/prompt versions, corpus size, k/temperature); versioned harness; triggers for re-evaluation (model/prompt/corpus/parameters/context/time); formal deviation handling.
9. Taxonomy of acceptance criteria
   - Retrieval & Grounding: recall@k, MRR; tests include distractors, follow-ups, and known-absent items to detect hallucination.
   - Faithfulness: ungrounded-citation rate ≤ threshold (e.g., ≤1%); automated detection via structured citations checked against context.
   - Reproducibility: byte-identical artifacts under fixed seeds/params where applicable.
   - Boundary: refusal of prohibited actions enforced by schema/guardrails.
   - Human-in-the-loop: 100% of low-confidence items receive reviewer disposition; role separation enforced.
   - Authority & Precedence: correct prioritization of binding rules over guidance with 100% pass on labeled authority pairs.
10. Human accountability and evidence credibility
    - Humans retain legal responsibility; design workflows to capture decisions, rationales, roles, and timestamps; prevent blind acceptance.
    - Every metric must declare population, time window, operating conditions (model, prompt, retrieval parameters), and known gaps; otherwise it’s a claim, not evidence.
    - Evidence has a shelf life; schedule periodic re-evaluation per lifecycle principles.
11. Invalidation factors and re-validation triggers
    - Model served version change; prompt change; corpus/knowledge-base change; retrieval parameter changes (k, weights, fusion, chunking); context-of-use shifts; elapsed time/drift. Record and control each; any trigger returns the system to measurement and re-qualification.
12. Practical retrieval considerations
    - Chunking strategies differ by document type (PDF, text, spreadsheets); choices affect ranking, precision, recall; document and qualify impacts; track corpus growth and tagging quality.
13. LLMs grading LLMs: limits and calibration
    - 2026 studies show ~76% inter-judge agreement and unreliability across safety/bias; LLM-as-judge is not acceptance evidence.
    - If used, pre-calibrate against 30–50 hand-adjudicated cases; use as triage only, with human adjudication for consequential decisions.
14. Continuous validation loop
    - Define context of use; build and version labeled sets under change control; declare risk-justified thresholds; measure baseline and each run; attach run records to releases; monitor for drift/provider changes; re-measure on triggers; maintain PCCP for adaptive systems; qualify evaluation tools.
15. Inspection-ready validation package (database-first)
    - Labeled test set with rationales; run records (date, harness commit, corpus size/composition, operator); per-call provenance (requested vs. served model, prompt hash, token counts); grounding exception log; stored output artifacts with immutable lineage; human review dispositions (append-only); threshold rationale tied to risk; re-qualification records for each trigger. Deliver as an interactive dashboard enabling drill-down.

## Assignments

- Define context of use for each AI system; align acceptance criteria to role, scope, and risk.
- Build, version, and place labeled test sets (cases, labels, parameters) under change control; include distractors, follow-ups, and known-absent items.
- Derive defensible thresholds based on influence × consequence and fit-for-use metrics; document rationales the team can defend.
- Establish structured acceptance criteria using the full anatomy (set, metric, threshold, conditions, harness, triggers, disposition).
- Measure baselines and subsequent runs; attach dated run records to release decisions.
- Implement continuous monitoring for drift, corpus changes (RAG), provider model changes, and prompt/parameter tweaks; define triggers for re-qualification.
- Qualify evaluation harnesses/tools; maintain qualification records.
- Classify systems on Fixed/Adaptive × Opaque/Inspectable; set validation strategies and re-validation cadence accordingly; establish PCCPs for adaptive algorithms.
- Update SOPs to reflect continuous validation, run-based evidence, deviation handling, and batch investigation extensions.
- Enforce human-in-the-loop checkpoints for critical decisions with recorded rationales and role separation.
- Standardize metric reporting with population, time window, operating conditions, and known gaps.
- Implement configuration management: hash/version prompts; log served model versions; record corpus composition and retrieval parameters each run.
- Calibrate any LLM judge and restrict use to triage; require human adjudication for acceptance evidence.
- Audit and document chunking strategies across document types; test impacts on retrieval quality.
- Re-check EMA/FDA guidance status before audits or talks (e.g., Annex 22 timelines; FDA AI guidance updates).
