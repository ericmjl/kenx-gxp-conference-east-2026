## Summary

Talk recorded at the GxP and AI conference Eric is attending this week. The presenter, Ben O'Brien (Phanero), frames the Intended Use Statement as the governance foundation of a GxP AI implementation: more than a traditional URS (a tick-box verification baseline), it is the control lever for risk tiering, validation depth, and inspection scope, and it is being socialized by FDA, EMA, and ISPE, whose Guide on AI he calls the best available guidance for getting GxP AI models into production. His best-practice structure has four pillars: Core Task (the what, with precise verbs instead of generic ones like define, categorize, or classify), Data Bounding Box (the where, what data goes in and out), Model Limitations (what it is not, so an inspector cannot infer unstated capabilities you never proved), and Governance Boundaries (the who, where the AI-to-human handoff happens and model validation effectively ends). Changing any pillar midstream triggers a governance domino effect that invalidates risk classification (model influence and decision consequence), data lineage and operational monitoring, metric alignment, and even training-data relevance (his oncology example: dropping the domain can make all gathered data useless), which is why agile iteration is fine for model development but breaks down in the governed validation phase.

The practical core is that wording determines regulatory burden: the same underlying model reads high-risk as "categorizes and assigns severity to incoming deviations" and low-risk as "drafts preliminary deviation summaries for QA review," so narrowing the intended use shrinks the auditable surface area you must defend. Bounded inputs (his computer vision example: images from cameras X, Y, Z on line one at standardized 5000k LED illumination) enable pre-validation gating that rejects non-conforming data before the model sees it, cutting a potential multi-year validation effort down to months and simplifying monitoring, while advisory outputs shift effort from proving the model near-perfect to a trained human, whereas autonomous outputs demand harder confidence scoring, fallback logic, and failure mode containment. He calls "utilising human oversight during operational execution" camouflage for passive rubber-stamping that feeds automation bias; inspectors will eventually pull that thread (training records, where the decision was actually made), so wording should mandate explicit QA review and electronic sign-off as a hard stop before execution. Metrics framing encodes the engineering-vs-quality tension (engineering tunes down false positives, quality would rather absorb false positives than let a false negative through; precision and recall are reasonably inversely proportional, F1 as the balance), and midstream scope shifts can flip which metrics matter. He separates routine maintenance retraining (adding a thousand new clear-glass-vial images under SOP and change control) from changes that break the qualified state (moving to amber glass vials requires full revalidation), endorses Google-style model cards as SOP appendices, and argues AI model validation (evaluated against intended use in the real-world environment; GAMP categories do not transfer) should be separated from traditional computerized system validation. He closes with a webinar plug: Victor Beckman, a Danish Medicines Agency inspector on the Annex 22 authoring group, discussing LLMs in a GxP context on October 22, and in Q&A adds that SaaS-style vendor documentation for AI models should mature the same way SaaS documentation did under CSA.

## Knowledge Points

### 1. Role and Definition of an Intended Use Statement

- Intended Use: An explicit declaration of an AI model’s operational scope, decision boundaries, and validated capabilities; it acts as the model’s “North Star.”
- Goes beyond traditional requirements by embedding decision context, autonomy limits, data scope, and governance anchors.
- Regulatory anchor for risk tiering, validation depth, and inspection scope; informs downstream deliverables and oversight expectations.

### 2. Traditional URS/CSA vs. GxP AI Intended Use

- Logic: Traditional systems are deterministic; AI is probabilistic with confidence scores and non-linear outputs.
- Scope: Traditional focuses on features/workflows; Intended Use centers on decision context, operational boundaries, and autonomy limits.
- Change mechanics: Traditional changes are localized; Intended Use shifts can have systemic impacts, invalidating upstream risk and data baselines.
- Governance: URS is a verification baseline; Intended Use is the primary governance control affecting risk classification and validation intensity.
- Validation separation: Validate the computerized system (access control, audit trails) using established methods; validate the AI model separately against its Intended Use, not via scripted CSA-style tests.

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">3. Structuring the Intended Use Statement: The Four Pillars</mark>

- Core Task (What): Precisely define the model’s operational scope and decision boundaries; avoid vague verbs; early clarity reduces validation burden.
- Data Bounding Box (Where): Explicitly define input data scope, environment, and physical attributes (e.g., device IDs, resolution, lighting, formats) to enable pre-validation gates and predictable monitoring.
- Model Limitations (What It Is Not): Document capability boundaries and explicit prohibitions to prevent inferred capabilities, especially for versatile models like LLMs.
- Governance Boundaries (Who): Define autonomy limits and human-in-the-loop roles; clarify AI-to-human handoff and where model validation ends relative to broader process risk.

### 4. AI Oversight and Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)

- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Rubber-stamping risk: Passive human review fosters automation bias and compliance gaps; inspectors are expected to scrutinize this more deeply.</mark>
- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Active verification: Require a hard stop with explicit QA review and electronic sign-off before execution; specify human actions to create auditable evidence and maintain advisory classification.</mark>

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">5. Governance Domino Effect of Midstream Changes</mark>

- Risk classification: Scope expansion increases Model Influence and Decision Consequence, voiding initial assessments.
- Data lineage and monitoring: Changing boundaries invalidates training datasets and monitoring strategies, necessitating redesign.
- Metric realignment: Scope shifts change which metrics matter (precision vs. recall, F1), potentially requiring retraining.
- Model training: Domain shifts (e.g., oncology to another area) render original data irrelevant, triggering new data evaluation and full revalidation.
- Agile limits: Iterative model development is fine; revisiting Intended Use post-validation causes cascading invalidations, making agile unsuitable for governed validation phases.

### 6. Risk Governance: Framing to Reduce Regulatory Burden

- Axes: Model Influence (decision-driving) and Decision Consequence (impact severity) govern regulatory risk.
- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Wording and perceived risk: Narrow, advisory phrasing lowers risk and validation burden (e.</mark>g., “drafts preliminary summaries for QA review” vs. “categorizes and assigns severity”).

### 7. Operational Control: Inputs (Bounded vs. Unbounded)

- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Bounded inputs: Pre-validation gating rejects non-conforming data; limits validation scope to predictable parameters and simplifies monitoring.</mark>
- Unbounded inputs: Require complex detection architectures to filter variations; broaden testing across permutations (lighting, devices), expanding validation timelines and risk.

[](plaud://image?type=summaryCard&id=oc_su_a2a766746d2d6e0756d4671776f808db_i10)

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">8. Operational Control: Outputs (Autonomous vs. Advisory)</mark>

- Autonomous: Requires strict confidence gating, fallback logic, failure containment, and safety proofs; places full regulatory liability on algorithmic validation and demands exhaustive testing.
- Advisory: Routes predictions to structured human review queues with confidence metadata; anchors liability in human QA SOPs and reduces technical validation complexity.

### <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">9. Operational Controls and Performance Metrics</mark>

- Framing dictates metrics and error tolerances; high-consequence tasks may require near-zero false negatives.
- Resolve precision vs. recall conflicts between engineering and quality by explicitly prioritizing metrics in the Intended Use; F1 is a balance but priorities must be stated.
- Scope changes alter acceptance criteria and invalidate baselines, requiring re-testing.

### 10. AI Model Documentation and Vendor Responsibility

- Vendor documentation evolution: AI model documentation is expected to improve with user feedback, analogous to SaaS documentation maturation.
- Model cards: Recommended to document model function, training, technology, and intended use; while not perfectly tailored to GxP, they improve understanding and risk assessment and can be appended to SOPs.
- SaaS provider role: Proactively supply detailed, non-proprietary model information to reduce burden on quality teams and enable defensible evaluation.

### 11. Practical Considerations for Validation and Lifecycle Governance

- Stakeholder alignment: Non-validation stakeholders must internalize Intended Use constraints to avoid rework and delays; define and communicate Intended Use first.
- Monitoring, oversight, and handoffs: Clearly bookend AI-to-human handoff; model validation may end there, but process risk assessment can extend beyond.
- <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Retraining boundaries: Distinguish routine maintenance (in-scope data updates via qualified SOPs and regression testing) from out-of-scope changes that break qualification and require full revalidation.</mark>
