Her central point was that **AI expands the control surface beyond the validated system to the full path from information to action**. <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Quality and security should therefore co-design controls early rather than conduct separate reviews after a solution has been built.</mark>

### Career shift revealed common ground between quality and cybersecurity

Arora began in R&D IT delivery. She worked on CTMS, regulatory information management, safety, and other platforms that had to comply with GXP. She learned compliance so she could make better delivery decisions without repeatedly asking quality for permission.

In 2016, her CIO directed her to take over global compliance. Her experience was then concentrated in GXP, but the new role also covered SOX and cyber controls. She initially disliked the move and continued to favor the field she knew best. Over time, cybersecurity became a passion.

That experience shaped the presentation:

- **RxT’s role**: RxT helps large and small pharmaceutical clients determine which tools fit their needs. Arora said the same AI tool can work well for one company and poorly for another.
- **Control-framework reality**: She found that a common control framework was not as common as its name implied. Different functions often used distinct terms and methods for controls that served similar purposes.
- **Cross-functional lesson**: Quality and cybersecurity often ask the same questions in different language. The answers may drive different actions, but both fields seek testable assurance.
- **Learning across disciplines**: Arora adopted risk-and-control matrices and control-effectiveness methods from her SOX work. She now applies those methods to quality instead of relying only on SOPs and periodic reviews.
- **Shared responsibility**: Patient safety, data integrity, and cybersecurity may each have a department that owns policy or expertise. Operational responsibility still belongs to everyone who handles the process or data.

<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Arora stressed that </mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">a cyber event can also be a quality event, and a quality event can become a cyber event</mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">.</mark> If a security failure changes a validated outcome, quality must be involved. In her earlier experience, cybersecurity performed reconnaissance while quality opened a deviation. Each function worked alone, then quality struggled to obtain the security findings needed for its investigation.

### AI expands risk across identities, data, models, and monitoring

Traditional validation focused on a defined system and its intended use. Security supplied access, detection, and protection controls around that system. AI changes the scope only somewhat, Arora said, but it changes where controls must be placed. Teams must protect the entire route from source information to an automated or human action.

The main risks span several domains:

- **Identity and access**: Organizations are creating agents rapidly. Some builders assume an agent does not need quality or cybersecurity review. This creates privilege misuse, identity mismatches, and excessive access.
- **Vendor access**: Vendors form an extended network that needs tighter management. Their presence increases the number of identities, tools, and connections within the control environment.
- **Prompt governance**: Prompt changes can alter behavior and should remain under change control when they affect validated use.
- **Data integrity**: Inputs can be tampered with deliberately or changed unintentionally. Good intentions do not establish lineage or prove that data remained intact.
- **Model change**: A model may change without the organization detecting it. External changes, including changes made by OpenAI, can alter behavior and require a controlled response.
- **Training drift**: Drift occurs over time, while many organizations still lack mature ways to control it.
- **Visibility**: Audit-log gaps and weak monitoring limit oversight. AI increases transaction volume, data load, and integration exposure.
- **Continuous operation**: Historic controls often tested a system at one point in time. AI agents can run 24/7, so verification and monitoring must become continuous.

Arora considered **excess permissions one of the most serious current problems**. Identity and access management was already difficult when she worked on a SailPoint implementation 10 years earlier. The larger number of agents, vendors, and connections has made the issue harder.

<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Bad source data illustrates why security can change the GXP state.</mark> A bad actor could inject poor clinical data. If an AI system ingests it, later decisions will also be bad and may create reputational harm. Similar harm can follow from undetected model changes or improperly granted permissions.

### Co-design should replace serial governance handoffs

Arora described the common operating model as a chain of disconnected handoffs. Quality provides GXP controls, security provides a larger set of NIST-based requirements, and IT adds technical controls. The end user receives overlapping demands separately because the control owners report through different organizations.

At the same time, executives press employees to innovate. Arora has heard top-down goals requiring AI use for **30% of a person’s job**, with metrics and bonuses tied to that target. People respond to incentives by finding more uses for AI. Legal, privacy, security, quality, and IT then approach those projects with separate approval requirements. The result feels like a barrier to innovation.

Arora proposed a co-design model with defined responsibilities:

- **Business**: Define and own the intended use.
- **Quality**: Assure the regulated use and establish the quality boundary.
- **Security**: Protect assets, patients, identities, and data.
- **IT or the vendor**: Own technical controls and change control.
- **All control owners**: Design and test a shared solution rather than passing it from one review to another.

Eric Ma noted that organizational design contributes to the problem. He also cited complaints that each party lacks enough time to work together. Arora agreed that time is a constraint. Yet separate reviews force the end user to visit each control owner and collect approvals like marks on a bingo card.

Her suggested model was to protect a recurring block, such as **two hours every Thursday**, when all control owners sit together. Project teams could bring ideas for an early, informal control conversation. The session would resemble a pre-ARD but function as a working discussion rather than a review board. Participants would assess intended use, fit-for-purpose controls, and risks before commitments harden.

This model also requires a behavioral change. Control owners cannot claim to enable innovation while handing a project 99 quality controls and another 99 security controls, then making approval slow and difficult. The organization’s shared goal is to bring products to patients faster and more safely.

Arora has experienced both models. <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">She described the difference between throwing work over the wall and co-creating around one shared design record as dramatic.</mark> **The best time to shape the control environment is before a statement of work has been signed and the solution has been built.** Pulling quality in late leaves it little choice but to impose changes that delay the project.

The right partner also depends on organizational culture. A relaxed advisor may work well in one company, while a stricter approach may suit another. Arora recommended choosing partners whose style fits the organization.

### One control design can serve several compliance languages

Quality and security often describe equivalent safeguards with different terms. Translating those terms improves collaboration:

- **<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Least privilege and segregation of duties</mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">: Both restrict what a user may do.</mark> A reviewer who cannot approve an item is constrained by role and authority.
- **MFA**: Security says multifactor authentication is required. Quality describes the outcome as ensuring that only the right person can approve a GXP action.
- **RBAC**: Role-based access control means only qualified users can perform regulated activities.
- **Audit trails**: Quality is not alone in needing them. Security can aggregate audit trails across systems and analyze them with advanced technology.
- **E-signatures and approvals**: Both disciplines need reliable identity, authorization, and evidence.
- **AI change governance**: Both need controlled versions and testable changes across prompts, models, tools, and connections.

Arora built a **50-control matrix for the EU AI Act**. She mapped it against the FDA’s seven-step AI material, NIST, and some SOX requirements. Not every control applies to every use case, but the matrix reduces duplicate work. She contrasted it with an earlier reference to Steve’s framework, whose stated count she remembered as either 120 or 190 controls.

The aim is one testable design. Quality asks how to validate it. Security asks how to protect it. <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">A shared framework lets each function use the resulting evidence for its own purpose.</mark>

### Batch-record review shows how controls must follow the workflow

<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Arora used a GXP batch-review scenario to show what protection by design requires.</mark> <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">The approved intended use was for AI to read approved batch records, draft exceptions with source references, and send the output to a qualified human expert.</mark> The human must have actual batch-record expertise. Merely placing an unspecified person “in the loop” is not enough.

The intended workflow preserves traceability:

1. AI reads authorized batch records.
2. It drafts exceptions from approved evidence.
3. It cites the supporting sources.
4. A qualified human reviews the result.

That design can fail if a team treats the AI as a simple business enabler and bypasses security or quality.

- **Source boundaries**: The agent should read only task-approved sources within the user’s permissions. Draft, obsolete, or irrelevant batch records must be blocked because they can produce false exceptions.
- **Read-only enforcement**: Saying a source is read-only does not make it so. The permission must prevent modification.
- **Change authority**: Only authorized roles should change models, system prompts, connections, or related settings. Uncontrolled changes can alter the data or outcome.
- **Rounding and manipulation**: A person or tool could round a number until it falls within acceptable limits and no longer appears out of specification. The change may be intentional or unintentional.
- **Version control**: Changes require controlled versions and independent approval.
- **<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">Human approval</mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">: A named, qualified person should authenticate, preferably through MFA, and approve within the batch system.</mark> **AI should have no signing or release rights.**
- **Service identity**: Every AI agent should be recorded in the CMDB with a defined identity, purpose, and permitted actions.
- **Credential limits**: Credentials should cover only the task at hand. Tool and API permissions must prevent the agent from editing, closing, or releasing batches.
- **Transaction controls**: The organization should check every action in the batch system, not merely inspect the prompt.
- **Continuous monitoring**: Controls must watch the running service rather than rely on point-in-time testing.

Arora summarized the division of labor as: **quality defines the boundary, cybersecurity and IT enforce it, and the functions test it together**.

### Deviation investigations need protected evidence and expert review

AI can also support deviation investigations and trend analysis, but polished language can conceal wrong content. Arora said Claude is especially capable of producing text that looks technically correct even when the underlying content is wrong.

In a deviation narrative, AI could downplay human error, reflect bias, or omit critical facts. The result might sound authoritative while avoiding a costly CAPA. The control response has three parts:

- **Human expertise**: A qualified reviewer checks the AI’s account against the evidence.
- **Quality controls**: Independent approval and regulated process controls govern the result.
- **Cybersecurity controls**: Original evidence remains protected from alteration.

If the source evidence has changed, human review cannot recover a trustworthy account. Arora’s broader warning was that wider AI adoption can also multiply AI-related failures unless these three safeguards expand with it.

### Final takeaway: test trust across the full control surface

<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">AI has enlarged the control surface to include </mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">models, prompts, data, tools, and outputs</mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">.</mark> All of them must remain controlled. Validation can no longer stop at testing whether a system performs its intended function. Teams must also test whether identities, permissions, evidence, and changes are trustworthy.

Arora said common security terminology made collaboration easier once she learned it. Using terms such as MFA, RBAC, and control surface helped her communicate with security, legal, privacy, finance, and SOX specialists without changing the underlying quality objective.

She closed with an example of excessive complexity. In an earlier system, she recalled more than 200 roles in TruPlus. The role matrix became so complicated that the team did not know how to test it. Cybersecurity partners then analyzed the design and helped simplify it. <mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">The example reinforced her main conclusion: </mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">quality and security become more effective when they treat each other as design partners rather than separate approval gates</mark>**<mark style="background-color: #F9EDD4">.</mark>
